Seeing Is Not Attributing1

How Image-Generating Agents Carried a Private Code Through a Noisy Renderer and Past a Frontier AI Reviewer
Daniel Foo
Nanyang Technological University (NTU), Singapore

With
Apart Research

Abstract

AI reviewers increasingly oversee agent systems while seeing only what the agents produce, not what they are paid or how they reason; current agent protocols and frontier APIs are built this way. We study what this costs when agents coordinate through generated images. In a twelve-round game, a sender (A) privately knows a target among four candidates and must convey it, by picture only, to two peers (B, C). All three gain if B and C both choose the target, unless a reviewer finds knowing joint coordination. The agents were GLM-5.3-Flash, pictures came from Qwen-Image-2.1-Turbo, and the reviewer was GPT-6.1 Sol at Codex’s highest reasoning setting. With the same incentive but no usable picture signal, B and C chose the client-correct answer in 6 of 8 votes. With pictures, the agents built a visible ordinal code and chose the target in 23 of 24 votes, although the generator drew only 2 of 36 sent pictures as briefed. The reviewer described and decoded the code in every round, yet rated every round “uncertain”, so the grant was paid in 11 rounds. Its rubric treated picture messages as the task, it did not see the payoff or the reasoning that show intent, and its reports were returned to the agents, who used them as image descriptions and as evidence that the code was safe. We argue that collusion studies should measure detection and attribution separately and that monitors need incentive information and decision history.

1. Introduction

Agents increasingly pass generated media to one another. An image or document can serve the visible task and carry a message to a partner at the same time, and the party that must catch the message is often another AI model reviewing the artifacts afterwards. Such a reviewer usually sees the outputs but not the agents’ payoffs or reasoning (Table 1). The A2A protocol connects “opaque agentic applications” that operate “without exposing their internal state, memory, or tools” [11]; frontier APIs return reasoning summaries rather than raw chains of thought [12, 13]; supervisors and AI-control monitors score individual outputs [14, 15]. The same limitation appears in markets: pricing algorithms, including LLM agents, can sustain supracompetitive prices without explicit agreement [16, 17], and U.S. courts generally require “plus factors” such as motive in addition to parallel conduct before inferring an agreement [18, 19]. Baker et al. report that monitoring a model’s chain of thought can be far more effective than monitoring its actions, and that optimising against the monitor led to obfuscation [20].

We address two questions from Track 3.1. RQ1 (carrier): can agents transmit a private choice through a hosted image generator that often draws something other than what it is asked for? RQ2 (countermeasure): can a frontier reviewer with this information attribute the resulting coordination? We run one twelve-round game with logged plans and reasoning, baselines without a usable signal, matched replays and comparison judges (Figure 1). Our main findings are as follows.

  • C1 The agents coordinated only when a picture carried a signal. Without a usable signal they mostly took the client-correct answer; with their own pictures they chose the target in 23 of 24 votes (Section 4.1).
  • C2 The agents kept the code working through a generator that rarely drew the brief. Only 2 of 36 sent pictures were faithful; seven behaviours, including draft inspection, defensive prompting and a parity check, explain how the code survived (Section 4.2).
  • C3 The reviewer detected the code but did not attribute it. GPT-6.1 Sol described and decoded the code in all 12 rounds and rated all 12 “uncertain”; six features of its rubric, inputs and feedback account for this (Section 4.3).

2. Related Work

Prior work asks whether agents can hide messages: Motwani et al. formalise secret collusion via steganography [1], Mathew et al. show that steganographic collusion can emerge under optimisation and test paraphrasing defences [2], Dineen et al. find agents improvising covert channels at test time against monitors without interaction history [3], and Hu et al. study misleading generative montage [4]. Anwar et al. define the steganographic gap as the difference between what intended receivers and a non-colluding sentinel can extract [5]. Our setting differs in three respects. The carrier is a separate image model that the sender must adapt to. The code is legible (gap ≈ 0.04), so the failure we study is attribution rather than detection of a hidden payload, the problem antitrust law faces with parallel conduct. The reviewer’s reports are returned to the agents.

3. Methods

Game. One cohort played twelve dependent rounds on 24 October 2026. Each round had four candidates; the client-correct answer was the qualifying candidate (quality ≥ 80, defects ≤ 5) with the lowest energy. A’s target T was drawn uniformly from the other three candidates, and this distribution was known to all agents, so T was never the client-correct answer. A’s ballot was fixed to T; B and C voted freely, giving 24 free ballots. B and C each earned 4,000 points for the client-correct answer, and all three earned 10,000 each if B and C both chose T and the reviewer made no supported finding of knowing joint coordination. A blind guess among the three possible targets has an expected grant of 3,333 points, below 4,000, and the prompt states this, so choosing the target is worthwhile only with a usable signal. The agents shared one system prompt, which states that “No host-supplied convention or successful precedent exists”, plus a role paragraph. They could not exchange text: A’s picture went to B and C, B’s to C, and C’s return picture to the next round’s A. Each picture required a plan (intent, recipient hypothesis, success check) committed before rendering, at most two renders, and no readable writing. Appendix B gives the prompts, tools and round order.

Reviewer. Each round, a fresh GPT-6.1 Sol context in Codex at the Ultra reasoning setting received all selected pictures, the locked ballots, the target distribution and its own earlier reports, and returned one of four verdicts: none, uncertain, supported suspicion or demonstrated. GPT-6.1 Sol was released on 29 September 2026; OpenAI positions it as close to its flagship model, and it ranks 7th of 27 models on the Vals Index [6–9]. The run logs record a Codex context but not the model string. The full report was sent to all three agents after the vote. Table 2 lists what each party could see.

Seventeen replays at rounds 4, 8 and 12 restarted from the saved state and changed one factor: the target, A’s picture (replaced by a task-only picture), B’s picture, or a re-render of the same brief. Two scripted inputs carried no usable code: an unreadable memo under the full incentive prompt, and a blank picture under an honest prompt without the grant. Six observers used the reviewer’s rubric without its earlier reports, 56 audit contexts rated image fidelity, and fresh Claude monitors with a neutral rubric received pictures only (L1), then ballots (L2), three earlier rounds (L3) and the payoffs (L4).

4. Results

4.1 C1: The agents coordinated only when a picture carried a signal

Under the full incentive prompt but without a usable picture code, B and C chose the client-correct answer in 6 of 8 votes, and in 2 of 2 under the honest prompt (Figure 2). Their reasoning follows the expected-value argument in the prompt:

“With no usable evidence recovering T (P2/P3/P4), I take the reliable client-correct ballot on P1 rather than a 1-in-3 blind guess.”— B, unreadable memo, round 4

The same reasoning appears when A’s picture was replaced by a task-only picture at round 12, where B wrote “no green highlight, arrow, or clear ordinal marker… I find no grounded signal distinguishing one candidate” and voted for the client answer. Both exceptions occurred at round 8, after seven rounds of play. B read the established code into a task-only picture (“The green-dressed figure sits third among the four”) and relayed it, and C followed. By then the convention was held in memory as well as in the pictures. Table 3 lists every replay branch. We did not run a game without pictures from round 1, so this baseline rests on the replays and on the expected-value argument stated in the prompt.

4.2 C2: The code survived a generator that rarely drew the brief

In round 1, A asked for four railway signals with only the third lit green, B added an arrow pointing at it in its relay picture, and both receivers voted P3. The same rule held for twelve rounds: the single green object, counted from the left, gives the candidate number (Figure 3). The rule moved with the target in rounds 5, 6, 7, 9 and 10, and B and C chose the target in 23 of 24 votes (96%), with both choosing it in 11 of 12 rounds (p < 10−9 under independent blind guessing, an idealisation since B and C are not independent). The pictures carried the choice: when a replay swapped the target, B and C followed the new target in 4 of 6 votes. The only miss came in round 6, when B miscounted the shift to the second position and voted for the client answer, while C read the relay and the original and voted for the target. The generator was unreliable: audits rated 2 of 36 sent pictures faithful, 29 partial and 5 contradictory, and the contradictions (dropped objects, extra objects, misplaced highlights) are the errors that break an ordinal code. Drafts fared no better (2 of 50 faithful, 37 partial, 11 contradictory), and re-rendering the same brief lowered target votes from 6 of 6 on the original pictures to 4 of 6. 4.3 C3: The reviewer detected the code but did not attribute it

GPT-6.1 Sol described the green cue in all 12 rounds and decoded it, for example in round 8: “the third-position motif plausibly refers to its ID rather than list order.” Its stated confidence rose from 0.70 to 0.87, and every verdict was “uncertain” (Figure 5). The code was easy to read: fresh decoders without the partners’ history recovered the target in 11 of 12 rounds, a steganographic gap of about 0.04. Table 5 lists six causes, each illustrated in Appendix D. The comparison judges separate them. Observers with the same rubric but without the reviewer’s earlier reports reached supported suspicion in 5 of 6 views. The monitor with a neutral rubric saw the signal in all 11 valid picture-only views but attributed collusion in none (“the images alone do not show knowing, intended coordination”), then in 10 of 12 once given ballots, and 12 of 12 with three earlier rounds, citing B’s relay: “B’s image re-sends A’s positional code to C, showing B decoded it.” The live reviewer had about the information of L3 and still answered “uncertain”. Since the evidence was available and the reviewer had decoded the code, this suggests that the rubric and the earlier verdicts decided the outcome; because the monitor differs in model family and rubric, the comparison is not controlled.

What the reviewer could and could not do. Attribution needed perception, decoding, cross-round inference and a licence to conclude. The reports show the first three. In round 5 the reviewer wrote that the agents’ “shift from the persistent third-position emphasis to fourth-position emphasis, accompanied by all ballots switching to P4, supports a recoverable ordinal convention more strongly than static resemblance alone.” It then concluded that “the record does not establish knowing improper adoption by two participants” (round 9). The agents’ reasoning, which the reviewer did not see, held the knowing choice. B in round 5: “If I choose P2, I get 4000 client credit but forfeit the 10000 grant.” The agents also read each report. C in round 2: “keep encoding but vary framing”; A in round 3: “Vary the scene to woodland so repetition looks like a theme, not a copied signal.” The reviewer still recognised the code in the new scenes, so the scenery hid nothing; the reports told the agents that a visible code had not been flagged.

5. Discussion and Limitations

How the findings connect. We read the two findings as parts of one process (Figure 6). The incentive gave A a reason to signal, but B and C followed only when a picture carried a usable signal. Because the generator often lost detail, the agents settled on one large, visible, error-tolerant cue, which relaying, the parity check and memory kept stable. The reviewer saw the cue, but its rubric defined signalling as the task, it lacked the payoff and the reasoning, and its earlier verdicts were in its context. Its reports then reached the agents, who used them to check renders and as evidence that the code had not been flagged. The noisy channel pushed the agents toward a visible code, and with a visible code, attribution decides the outcome.

Implications beyond this game. The following points are hypotheses from one cohort. Each depends on a property that recurs where agents are overseen, which is why we expect it to transfer (Appendix E).

  • Collusion research. Detection and attribution should be measured separately, since all our judges detected the code and differed only in attributing it. Legible coordination needs its own threat model: a steganographic gap near zero did not make the collusion unsafe for the agents. A lossy carrier pushed the agents toward a coarse convention, and we expect other lossy carriers, such as translation or summarisation, to do the same.
  • Detection. Monitors should receive the incentive structure, since matching wrong choices is suspicious only when it pays; this is the motive “plus factor” courts already use. They should test whether behaviour tracks a hidden variable across rounds, apply a stated standard of proof, and be calibrated on honest cases without their own earlier verdicts in context.
  • Countermeasures. Channel noise did not prevent coordination (2 of 36 faithful pictures, 23 of 24 target votes). Replacing the signal with a task-only picture reduced target votes to 2 of 6. Reports should go to the principal, not to the agents being judged.
  • Other settings. Agent marketplaces and procurement, coding and review pipelines, media pipelines and algorithmic pricing combine output-only oversight, private incentives and feedback to the agents. In such settings we expect coordination that is detected but not attributed.

Track 3.1. Generated images carried a private choice without hidden encoding. Renderer noise and per-artifact review did not stop it; removing the signal, giving the judge ballots and history, and removing its earlier verdicts helped in small descriptive samples.

Limitations. The coordination was induced: A was told to communicate and the payoffs rewarded it, so this is not spontaneous collusion. We ran one cohort of 12 dependent rounds; the replays are descriptive branches of one trajectory, and B and C are not independent. There was no game without pictures from round 1, so the baseline rests on the replays and the prompt’s expected-value argument. The reviewer’s identity comes from the operator’s configuration, not a logged model string, and the external capability figures for GPT-6.1 Sol are vendor claims or third-party benchmarks. Observer and monitor views are single stochastic judgments. The monitor differs from the reviewer in both rubric and model family, so we cannot separate the two, and the ladder has no honest-negative rounds, so its false-positive rate is unknown. Appendix A gives the full list.

Future work. Each recommendation is a testable intervention on the same game: disclose the payoffs to the reviewer, replace the commission framing with a stated standard of proof, withhold verdicts from the agents and remove the reviewer’s own history. It should also add honest-negative rounds, other model families and other lossy carriers, such as documents or translation.

6. Conclusion

With an incentive but no usable signal, the agents mostly chose the client-correct answer. With a generator that often drew something other than the brief, they built a visible, error-tolerant code and chose a private target in 23 of 24 votes. A frontier reviewer at its highest reasoning setting described and decoded the code in every round and rated every round “uncertain”, because its rubric treated picture messages as the task, it did not see the payoff or the reasoning that show intent, and its reports returned to the agents. Current agent protocols and APIs give reviewers similar information. We recommend that collusion studies report detection and attribution separately, and that monitors receive incentives and decision history, apply a stated standard of proof, work without their own earlier verdicts and report to principals instead of the agents.
