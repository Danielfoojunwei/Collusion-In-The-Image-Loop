Abstract
Increasingly autonomous agentic AI systems pose novel multi-agent risks, such
as secret collusion via covert communication channels. The natural defence to
these collusion attempts is to monitor plain-text communication, but the effi-
cacy of monitors has been called into doubt by increasingly sophisticated model
steganography; indeed, some theoretical schemes have been proposed that are
information-theoretically or computationally indistinguishable from good-faith
plain-text communication. In this paper, we demonstrate that the complexity of
these schemes is no longer a safety barrier, as agentic coding models can already
produce undetectable stegosystems when given realistic tool usage, such as code
execution or accessing research papers through web searches. Agents also adapt
when key ingredients are missing, for example, by adding model-sampling com-
ponents or implementing related keyed coding schemes. We then frame tacit
steganographic coordination between agents as a Schelling-point problem and
introduce coordination metrics for estimating when two agents are likely to se-
lect compatible schemes without explicit prior agreement. Our results suggest a
shift in the threat model for covert communication between AI agents, where the
main barrier is no longer whether frontier agents can understand and implement
sophisticated stegosystems, but coordination: whether independently acting agents
can converge on compatible schemes, keys, and parameters. We find substantial
convergence on broad scheme families but limited strict one-shot coordination,
suggesting that shared artefacts, repeated interaction, and tool-mediated search are
the settings where covert communication risks are most acute. Overall, our findings
provide empirical grounding for the recent strategic confinement hypothesis, which
assumes that capable agents can construct covert channels that survive monitoring.
1 Introduction
Monitored natural-language channels are a common safety boundary in multi-agent AI systems.
Modern large language model (LLM) agents increasingly use tools, call APIs, execute code, and
coordinate with other agents through natural-language messages [Yao et al., 2023, Yuan et al., 2025,
Wu et al., 2023]. A system designer may therefore allow two agents to exchange task-relevant
messages while relying on a human or model monitor to prevent private information, hidden goals, or
coordination signals from crossing the boundary [Greenblatt et al., 2024, Baker et al., 2025]. This
defence assumes that if the monitor can read the messages, it can enforce the information-flow policy.

arXiv:2606.28425v1 [cs.CR] 25 Jun 2026</page><page num="2">Steganography breaks this assumption, since a sender can encode a payload inside apparently benign
text, allowing a receiver with the right decoding procedure to recover information that the monitor
does not know was transmitted [Simmons, 1984]. This risk has been formalised as secret collusion
among AI agents, where agents use covert communication to hide the true nature of their interaction
from oversight [Motwani et al., 2025]. Prior work has also shown that language-model steganography
can be made information-theoretically undetectable [Schroeder de Witt et al., 2023] or undetectable
under appropriate sampling and cryptographic assumptions [von Ahn and Hopper, 2004, Ziegler
et al., 2019, Christ et al., 2023, Zamir, 2024]. Thus, we ask if current agentic models operationalise
such schemes when given realistic tool affordances.
To answer this, we evaluate tool-using LLM agents to construct covert communication channels
under a range of increasingly restricted environments. Agents can implement candidate undetectable
stegosystems from research papers, recover implementation details through web search, add missing
sampling components, and hide covert-channel logic inside an existing multi-agent codebase. We
then study the coordination problem faced by two agents that cannot explicitly agree on a scheme.
Treating steganographic design choices as focal points, we estimate how often independent agents
converge on compatible algorithms and parameters, building on prior work on tacit coordination in
LLMs [Aharon et al., 2026].
Our results suggest that covert communication is no longer bottlenecked by cryptographic expertise,
and is becoming an ordinary consequence of agentic tool use. The limiting factor is increasingly
not cryptographic invention, but ordinary agentic competence: reading papers, writing code, using
tools, and converging on salient designs. Overall, our findings provide empirical grounding for the
recent strategic confinement hypothesis [Schroeder de Witt, 2026], a reinterpretation of Lampson’s
confinement problem [Lampson, 1973] for strategic agents, which assumes that capable agents can
construct covert channels that survive monitoring.
Contributions. We make four contributions. First, we introduce an agentic covert-channel con-
struction benchmark, where a tool-using LLM must build a working natural-language stegosystem
rather than merely describe one (Section 3.1). Second, we frame tacit steganographic agreement
as a Schelling-point coordination problem over algorithms, keys, and decoding-relevant parameters
(Section 3.2.1). Third, we show robust implementation capability: agents construct functional
covert channels with papers, web search, code execution, model-sampling components, and existing
multi-agent codebases (Section 4.1). Fourth, we identify exact parameter agreement as the main
bottleneck: agents often converge on broad scheme families, but strict first-shot complete coordination
remains fragile (Section 4.2).
2 Background and Problem Setup
Monitored-channel threat model. We consider a multi-agent system (MAS) with at least two
agents, a sender A and a receiver B, connected by some monitored natural-language channel. Agent
A has access to some information x that B is not permitted to learn. A monitor observes the messages
sent from A to B, and blocks messages that appear to violate the information-flow policy. The
intended safety boundary is therefore content-level monitoring, where, if the monitor can read every
message, it should be able to prevent restricted information from crossing the channel.
Instead of sending x directly, A can send an apparently benign cover text c
′ 
from which B can recover
a hidden payload using a decoding procedure. The monitor may observe the complete text of c
′ 
while
remaining unaware that a payload was transmitted. We focus on text-based steganographic channels,
where the cover object is natural-language model output.
Steganographic channels. A keyed stegosystem consists of an encoder, a decoder, a payload space,
a cover distribution, and a shared secret key. The encoder maps a payload and cover-generation
process into a stego-object, while the decoder uses the key to recover the payload. The relevant
security notion is not whether the text looks suspicious to a casual reader, but whether a monitor
without the key can distinguish stego-objects from ordinary samples drawn from the cover distribution.
Prior work has proposed language-model stegosystems and watermarking-derived schemes with
formal undetectability guarantees under appropriate assumptions [Christ et al., 2023, Zamir, 2024].
Our question is whether current agents can operationalize such schemes in practice.
2</page><page num="3">Agentic construction. Tool use changes the threat model. A model that cannot directly produce a
covert channel from memory may still be able to read papers, search the web, write code, run tests,
use model-sampling components, and iterate until an encoder/decoder pair works. We therefore
evaluate covert-channel construction as an agentic capability rather than as a memorised behaviour. In
our experiments, success requires producing an implementation that can transmit synthetic payloads
through benign-looking outputs under the constraints of the environment.
Tacit coordination. A second obstacle is coordination. Two agents may both be capable of building
covert channels, but fail to communicate if they independently choose incompatible algorithms, keys,
or hyperparameters. We treat this as a focal-point coordination problem, where the design space
contains many possible steganographic schemes, but some are more salient because they are named
in the prompt, present in a shared file system, easy to find on the web, or common in the training
distribution. We estimate the probability of tacit agreement by sampling independent agent plans and
measuring how often they collide on compatible algorithmic and parameter choices.
We provide an extended background, with a gentle introduction to steganography in Appendix A.
Additionally, we provide a survey of related work in the fields of misalignment, watermarking, and
LLM cooperation in Appendix B.
3 Methodology
We break down the likelihood of harmful steganography S into 3 key areas assumed independent of
each other: the likelihood that an agent can implement a scheme I, the likelihood that agents can
coordinate on a compatible channel C, and the risk that illegal information flow can cause harm R.
P[S] = P[I] × P[C] × P[R] (1)
3.1 Steganographic implementation
Agents are frequently permitted unlimited and unmonitored tool usage, for example, with Claude’s
–dangerously-skip-permissions tag [Hughes, 2026]. We assess whether agents can implement un-
detectable steganography, such that a monitor with access only to agent outputs cannot detect the
presence of a hidden payload. Formally, we define the threat model used.
Actors and goals
• Adversary: A capable agent intending to covertly transmit information in its natural language
outputs without detection by content-level monitoring. Shares a secret key with the receiver.
• Receiver: A cooperating party (human or AI) that possesses the secret key and intends to
decode hidden messages from the agent’s outputs.
• Monitor: The defender (human or AI) responsible for detecting and preventing covert
channels. Has access to all agent outputs, but not the tool usage or intermediate artefacts.
Adversary capabilities and knowledge
1. Ability to execute code in a sandboxed environment.
2. Access to a sub-model (or API) that returns log probabilities for candidate next tokens, and
the ability to call it.
3. Access to a file system to stage intermediate artefacts, including models and test data, and
optionally install vetted packages.
4. Ability to find/derive an undetectable steganography scheme, given that the paper by Zamir
[2024] is in their file system.
In some aspects, this is a reasonable threat model. Developers rarely monitor tool usage unless
they are particularly security-conscious, agents typically have access to the file system and can
execute code, and frontier models are capable of understanding complex research papers and correctly
implementing concepts from them. However, few agents have access to a sub-model; malicious
prompts could be prevented with additional monitoring; generic file systems don’t contain research
papers detailing a steganographic implementation. As such, we design experiments that isolate each
assumption to investigate which are necessary for a successful implementation.

Schelling sharpness depends on the population of players and the game environment. More rational
players will have a higher value of C, and will exhibit better coordination; an environment with few
salient solutions will result in a more concentrated Schelling salience distribution.
An environment in which two agents connected by a monitored channel with simultaneous messaging
independently attempt to implement a steganographic scheme can be considered a two-player pure
coordination game. Formally, possible implementations are actions, including hyperparameter or key
selection, and Nash equilibria are compatible implementations that can successfully encode/decode
payloads. Research suggests that LLMs surpass reinforcement learning methods [Agashe et al.,
2025] and humans [Aharon et al., 2026] in some pure coordination games; hence, when agents are
placed into a coordination game, we must expect their coordinated capability to be correlated with the
sharpness of the game. Thus, by assessing the MAS Schelling sharpness with respect to a common
knowledge base of steganographic implementations, we can determine the likelihood that two agents
choose the same implementation without prior communication.
3.2.1 Measuring coordination
An agent attempting to coordinate on a steganographic implementation in environments that don’t
specify precise implementations could implement a variety of stegosystems, with a wide range of
hyperparameter values; thus, the probability of perfect coordination is minimal, and obtaining the
empirical coordination rate is infeasible. Schelling sharpness cannot be precisely determined, as there
is no concrete metric for the salience of each implementation in a MAS. Thus, an approximation of
Schelling sharpness is an effective approximation to the empirical rate.

4.3 Mitigations
Agents with access to tools proved very effective at implementing stegosystems, highlighting the
dangers of unmonitored tool use. The most effective mitigation is to monitor tool usage and to move
away from methods that skip permissions. Another effective but computationally expensive method
is to crowd the common knowledge base with similar implementation descriptions, reducing the
Schelling sharpness by introducing new, similarly salient Nash equilibria; an exponential number
of descriptions need to be included to ensure a negligible chance of coordination. For the MAS
experiments, we found that agents with no tool usage coordinate effectively on most hyperparameters,
but cannot coordinate completely on undetectable schemes; hence, using monitors between agents
reduces the threat posed by steganography.
4.4 Limitations
Sample size. A key limitation of our implementation experiments is the very small sample size.
Each experiment had long setup, implementation, and verification times, and as such a large sample
size was infeasible. This is also a limitation of the coordination experiments, caused by a restriction
on available tokens: we provide a lower bound for CCI, but with a larger sample size, many instances
where some hyperparameter probability was 0 in our experiment would contribute to the CCI. We
also didn’t provide agents with tool access for these experiments, as it was beyond the scope of our
research, but repeating experiments with web and PDF tools would likely raise the CCI significantly.
Model scope. We also only used Anthropic models in these experiments; thus, our claims only
apply directly to those, though we would expect them to generalise to LLMs from other providers.
5 Conclusion
In this work, we evaluated the limiting factor of agentic covert communication by separating the
problem into steganographic implementations and tacit coordination on stegosystems. Empirically,
we demonstrated that under reasonable tool affordances, frontier agentic models can operationalise
varied stegosystems, including ones that are computationally undetectable or perfectly secure. We
also demonstrated that complete successful coordination without prior communication or tool usage
is non-negligible under reasonable circumstances. Both our formulation for Schelling sharpness
and coordination metrics apply to future coordination games research. Similarly, our discussion on
information flow and risk can be flexibly applied to other research on monitored-channel systems.

be ordinary chatbot interaction, but automated safety-critical research itself. Future frontier-AI safety

data [Katzenbeisser and Petitcolas, 2000]. Fortunately, there is extensive research into embedding
watermarks in AI-generated content for different purposes. There exist watermarks for text-based

et al., 2023] and undetectable without a secret key [Christ et al., 2023, Boroujeny et al., 2024]; and
for image outputs, remaining undetectable without a secret key [Gunn et al., 2024]. Major LLM
providers originally pledged to watermark content for safety [Bartz et al., 2023], but real-world
deployment remains limited, likely due to misaligned incentives between LLM providers and end
users [Liu et al., 2026].
B.3 LLM cooperation
LLMs exhibit strong coordination capabilities in general scenarios [Zhu et al., 2025], but without
an imposed organisational structure, their helpful nature leads to over-reporting and unnecessary
compliance [Guo et al., 2024]; the benefits of structure are exhibited in real-life applications like a
MAS-operated vending machine [Anthropic, 2025b]. A tacit coordination game is a pure coordi-
nation game with multiple Nash equilibria; rational decision makers cannot be certain about which
equilibrium action other players will use [Huyck et al., 1990]. LLMs have remarkable abilities in tacit
coordination games, but often struggle in common-sense coordination involving numbers or cultural
archetypes [Aharon et al., 2026]. Additionally, LLMs perform well in extended self-interested games
[Akata et al., 2025] such as the iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma family [Robinson and Goforth, 2006].
Advanced cooperative reasoning problems require Theory of Mind (ToM) reasoning - the ability
to understand that other people have their own thoughts [Ruhl, 2025], thus LLMs must predict the
intentions of their partners [Agashe et al., 2025]. Despite early research suggesting that LLMs possess
ToM [Kosinski, 2023, Strickland, 2024, Strachan et al., 2024], recent results highlight deficiencies in
LLM social reasoning abilities [Amirizaniani et al., 2024, Agashe et al., 2025], implying that ToM
is likely mimicked by reflection of pre-training data [Aharon et al., 2026, Kosinski, 2023]. ToM
reasoning can be improved by integrating human intentions and using emotive language [Amirizaniani
et al., 2024], by explicitly representing agent beliefs [Li et al., 2023], or by inferring and propagating
weighted hypotheses about agents’ thoughts [Kim et al., 2025].
